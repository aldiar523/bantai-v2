A weekly list of public works contracts worth checking.

BantAI reads every contract in the DPWH project list each Monday and marks the open ones that an auditor, a journalist or a citizen group should look at first, with the reason and the source for each mark.

Goal

Point limited audit and reporting time at the contracts most likely to need it, while the work is still under way and the money is still being paid.

Every mark is a request to check. It is not a finding, and it never says that anyone did wrong.

Who it is for

Auditors and inspectorsA short list per region and office, with what to ask for: documents, dates, site checks.
JournalistsContracts that already carry an official record, and the documents to start from.
Civil society and community monitorsWhere open projects are, how far along they are said to be, and what to look for on site.

What it can and cannot do

It can

  • Read all 267,000 contracts in the DPWH list every week.
  • Show which open contracts already have an official record: an audit finding, a court case, a revoked licence.
  • Find contracts whose figures break a rule, such as an award above the approved budget.
  • Show where several independent warning signs point the same way.
  • Seal each week's list, so it can be checked later against what comes out.

It cannot

  • Say whether a contract is corrupt, or how likely it is.
  • See what is not in open data: payments, site reality, private agreements.
  • Prove yet that its warning signs predict wrongdoing: too few past cases carry a contract ID.
  • Correct DPWH's data. Statuses, dates and coordinates are as DPWH gives them.
  • Replace a site visit or a document check.

Each week

  1. Save the full DPWH project list and what changed since last week.
  2. Join each contract, by its ID, to PhilGEPS notices and awards, bid records, performance evaluations, PCAB licences and the register of documented problem contracts.
  3. Run every check on every open contract. Contracts reported 100% done count as complete, even if DPWH still lists them as on-going.
  4. Rank: mark A, then B, then C; larger contracts first; no office above 30% of the top 50.
  5. Seal the list with a SHA-256 fingerprint in the public ledger.
  6. Reviewers read the documents, may ask for an AI review, and record a decision. A person decides.

How reliable it is

Marks A and B rest on records and rules, so their reliability is a question of data quality, which reviewers check on documents. Mark C rests on how schemes work. We tested it against the problem contracts that can be tied to an ID; there are too few to show that it beats a random pick, so it is labelled "not yet shown". Two tempting methods did not work and are not used: automatic detection of change in satellite images, and detecting bid rotation among firms.

Each weekly list is sealed so that this can be tested forward, as new audits, cases and statements become public.

AI review

On request, a reviewer gets an AI review of one contract: a summary, ordinary explanations to rule out, what news, official statements and court records say, and the next document checks. Every outside claim links to a source. It advises; it never changes a mark or a decision, and only the review team sees it.

Data protection

  • Status words come from the source ("charged", "revoked"), never stronger.
  • No private individual is named: no company officers, owners or employees.
  • Marks B and C, AI reviews and decisions are encrypted and open only with the review team's key, in the reviewer's own browser.
  • Open, legal data only.
