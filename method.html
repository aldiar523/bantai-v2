How it works

How a contract gets on the list, and how far to trust it

Three kinds of flag, the reasoning and the law behind each, what can make them wrong, and what has been tested so far.

Plaridel bypass road works, Bulacan, 2017. Photo: Florentino Floro, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons.

The goal

Point people with limited time to the open public works contracts most worth checking, while the money can still be stopped or recovered.

For whom

State auditors, reporters and citizen monitors. The official records are public; the rest of the list is for a review team.

What a flag is

A request to check, with the reason and its source. It is never a finding, and it never says that anyone did wrong.

The three flags

Ordered by how much outside confirmation stands behind them

A contract gets the highest flag it qualifies for. A comes first because an official body has already acted. B comes next because the figures contradict a written rule. C comes last because it rests on BantAI's own reading of patterns. Within each flag, larger contracts come first.

Why it comes first

BantAI does not judge anything here. An auditor, a court, a regulator or DPWH itself has already acted, and the contract is still open. The question is simply whether that action reached this contract: who is doing the work now, and whether payments continue.

This is also why A is the only public flag. It repeats what an official source has already published, in the source's own words: "revoked", "charged", "listed". Most revocations on the list date from 1 September 2025, often well after the award.

How it can be wrong

  • A licence number is mistyped in the DPWH list, so the wrong firm is matched.
  • A revocation was lifted or appealed after PCAB published it.
  • The DPWH status is stale: the work is finished, or the contract was already terminated or reassigned.
  • A record names a project with a similar title in another year or office.
  • Two different firms share a name with a debarred one; that is why a person confirms every name-only match.
Check firstThe contractor's current PCAB status, and DPWH's record of the contract since the revocation: suspension, termination, reassignment, or payments made.

Why it comes second

No official has said anything yet, but no judgement about what is normal is needed either. The law sets the line, and the published figures are on the wrong side of it.

Two things can be true when that happens: a rule was broken, or a figure was entered wrongly. Either is worth knowing, and only the documents can tell them apart. That is why B stays with the review team until a document check of a sample shows that at least 70% are confirmed and fewer than 30% are data errors.

This week almost all B flags come from one check, the licence limit. The table of limits is being verified against PCAB's original before B is relied on.

How it can be wrong

  • A joint venture is recorded under one partner, whose category alone is too small.
  • The licence category or the amount is entered wrongly.
  • The award includes items the approved budget figure in PhilGEPS does not.
  • The limits table differs from PCAB's current one.
Check firstThe notice of award and the bid documents: the approved budget, the bid amount, who bid, and the licence (or joint-venture agreement) submitted at bidding.

Not counted here: the rule checks of flag B, which used to be a seventh risk family and only repeated B. Shown as context and never counted: the province's share of elected officials from political families. It is the same for every contract in a province, so it says nothing about one contract.

Why three, and why it comes last

Every sign on its own has an ordinary explanation. A remote site draws one bidder; a good firm wins a lot in a small office. But the ordinary explanation is different for each risk family. When three unrelated ones fire on the same contract, three ordinary explanations have to line up at once.

This is the logic of red-flag screening used in procurement research. Three is where the list stays workable: .

Tested against about 260 known problem contracts, contracts with two or more risk families were only 1.08 times as common as expected, which is no real difference. Too few known cases had three or more to test at all. So C rests on how schemes work, not on proof that it predicts them. That is why it comes last, why only the review team sees it, and why every weekly list is sealed: it will be tested against what comes out later.

How it can be wrong

  • The ordinary explanations do line up: a remote, flood-prone site with few able firms.
  • Risk families that look independent share one cause, such as a small local market.
  • Affiliation: two different people share a name, or the managing officer changed since the contract (the PCAB list shows the current one).
  • Price benchmarks ignore terrain, bridges or materials that justify a higher cost.
  • Site matching joins two different works at nearby coordinates.
Check firstWhich risk families fired and whether they share a cause. Then the documents for the strongest one: bids for tender, the cost estimate for price, past evaluations for the record.

Honest limits

What it can do, and what it cannot

It can

  • Read the whole DPWH project list every week.
  • Show which open contracts already carry an official record.
  • Find figures that appear to break a rule.
  • Show where several independent warning signs agree.
  • Seal each week's list, so it can be tested later.

It cannot

  • Say whether a contract is corrupt, or how likely it is.
  • See payments, the site itself, or private agreements.
  • Prove yet that its warning signs predict wrongdoing.
  • Correct DPWH's statuses, dates or locations.
  • Replace a site visit or a document check.

02

03

Rank

A, then B, then C; larger first; no office above 15 of the top 50.

05

Seal

Fix the list with a digital seal in the public ledger.

06

Review

People read the documents, may ask for an AI review, and decide.

AI review

A second look, on request

A reviewer can ask for an AI review of one contract: a summary, ordinary explanations to rule out, what news, official statements and court records say, and the next documents to request. Every outside claim links to its source.

It advises. It never changes a flag or a decision, never calls anyone corrupt, and only the review team sees it.
